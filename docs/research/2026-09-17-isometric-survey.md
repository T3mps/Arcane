# How isometric / 2.5D three-quarter games are built -- a survey

**Date:** 2026-09-17
**Purpose:** Input to the Arcane isometric-support decision. The engine today is one right-handed 3D world, +Y up, an orthographic 2D XY camera, a perspective editor camera landing in F4, meshes + sprites, and an id-buffer picker. The open choice is between (a) 2:1 sprite isometric in a 2D plane with a coordinate rule, (b) a true orthographic 3D camera on the iso diagonal over a 3D ground, and (c) a narrow-FOV perspective camera. Aphelyon is a grid-based isometric game with Wakfu-style in-place combat and sprite characters.
**Method:** Web search + fetch. Every claim carries a URL. "Dev-stated" = the developer or their official doc says it; "inferred" = my reading of screenshots, third-party analysis, or the math.

---

## 1. Shipped games and their technical model

**Diablo 2 (2000).** Fully 2D. Backgrounds are tiled 2D sprites and characters are pre-rendered sprites; the sprites are rendered from 3D models with a camera "looking down at 30 degrees and to the side by 45 degrees" so that the tiles keep a 2:1 pixel ratio (dimetric, on-screen axis angle arctan(1/2) ≈ 26.565°) [Wikipedia: Isometric video game graphics]. Depth handling is painter's-order draw of the 2D tiles/sprites (inferred from the 2D architecture). *Resurrected* (2021) confirms how thin the presentation layer is: it "overlays upgraded 3D models atop the original Diablo II's 2D sprites, and is designed to toggle between the legacy and upgraded visuals with a button press" (dev-stated via Wikipedia) -- i.e. the 2D grid logic never changed, only the renderer.

**Diablo 3 (2012) and Diablo 4 (2023).** Real-time 3D, perspective camera, fixed angle. D3 "uses a custom 3D game engine in order to present an overhead view ... somewhat similar to the isometric view used in previous games" [Wikipedia: Diablo III]. Players demonstrate the perspective projection empirically: in D1/D2 "pillars ... look the same in height and angle as you approach them," whereas in D3 "things farther away are a different angle and are smaller" [Blizzard forum thread] (inferred, but the evidence is unambiguous). Blizzard's stated reason for the locked angle: "wall cut-offs, cliff designs, background designs are all based on one fixed isometric view" [DiabloFans]. D4's art director framed the camera as recapturing "the gothic horror isometric camera view" of the first two games; D4 later shipped Standard/Far zoom presets [wowcarry.com] and its zoom is widely criticised as tight [Medium/Alex Rowe]. Depth = real depth buffer (inferred from real-time 3D).

**Path of Exile 1 / 2.** Custom engine, real-time 3D, fixed-angle camera. An independent calibration of PoE's projection from screenshots recovered a *perspective* projection matrix (the fit includes a homogeneous divide) with a camera at roughly (5.3, -4.9, 10.5) box-lengths from the origin, and shows that "the 3D position of the character can be recovered from the pixel coordinate ... assuming z = 0" [nicholastsmith.wordpress.com] (inferred; excellent example of picking under a fixed perspective camera). GGG's own statements only say the angle is chosen "to maximize visibility and spatial awareness" [lensviewing.com summarising GGG]. PoE2 keeps the same camera model [Wikipedia: Path of Exile 2].

**Divinity: Original Sin 2 (2017) and Baldur's Gate 3 (2023) -- Larian.** Real-time 3D, perspective, free camera. DOS2 lets you "zoom in and rotate the camera, which provides true 3D gameplay," with Top-down / Isometric / Dynamic / Free camera modes [Steam discussion]. BG3 runs on the Divinity engine, and "has a free-floating camera, with players able to adjust the perspective from top-down isometric to third person" [Wikipedia: BG3]; a Tactical View pans up to look straight down [dotesports]. There is no orthographic projection anywhere in this lineage (inferred).

**Pillars of Eternity / Deadfire (Obsidian, Unity).** *The* reference for pre-rendered 2D backgrounds + real-time 3D characters. Dev-stated, from Obsidian's Update #79: backgrounds "are rendered out of Maya as a 2D image"; Maya outputs "four passes: final, depth, normal, and albedo. These passes are combined together in Unity for per-pixel occlusion of 3D objects, and for real-time dynamic lighting"; "The illusion comes together only when an orthographic camera is placed at the perfect angle"; "dynamic deferred lights can affect the background and characters" [Obsidian Update #79]. A third-party analysis adds the key insight: the pre-rendered passes are literally a pre-baked G-buffer, so the game runs "as a Deferred Renderer without having to do runtime work to compute the G-Buffers," and "there is no extra work to place the characters and props into the correct depth ... because the depth information has also been provided" -- at the cost that ordinary shadow maps are impossible because "you can not render the scene from the perspective of the shadow casting light" [Projection Space]. **The depth-buffer trick:** the background quad writes the pre-rendered depth into the real depth buffer, so 3D characters are occluded per-pixel by the painting with no sprite sorting at all.

**Disco Elysium (ZA/UM, Unity).** Painted backgrounds + 3D characters, orthographic. Dev-stated: the team looked at the Pillars approach but "rejected a Pillars of Eternity-style backdrop approach due to lighting and visibility challenges. Instead, they built 'a simple 3D block-in world which gets a similar depth-occlusion shader'"; in Blender they build "a cuboid which represents the orthographic rendering camera space" and "project [UVs] from the screen plane to the mesh" so painted tiles line up exactly in Unity [ZA/UM devblog: Tiling of the World]. So the painting is camera-projected onto low-poly proxy geometry that writes real depth and receives real lights. Backgrounds "use rendered heightmaps," animated/pickable things are real-time overlays [ZA/UM devblog: From Render to Paintover]. Technical artist Siim Raidma's GDC 2020 talk "Turpentine Fumes and Shaders" covers "hand painted and dynamically lit backgrounds for an isometric RPG" [Game Developer]; art director Aleksander Rostov: "The isometric image is wonderful in that it is flat ... You can design the entire game as if it was a painting" [PC Gamer]. A UE forum thread trying to reproduce this confirms the general approach is "depth sorting and depth maps" with per-pixel occlusion [Epic forums].

**Hades / Hades II (Supergiant, custom engine).** Hades: hand-painted 2D environments, characters modelled in ZBrush/Maya and then pre-rendered to "thousands upon thousands of frames" of sprites from many angles [Game Developer; Supergiant-derived summaries]. Hades II: "one of the studio's first to feature fully 3D character models rendered in real-time, a brand-new pipeline," in "the custom, in-house rendering engine" [DigiPen]. Dev statements on projection are absent; the fixed three-quarter view and 2D world are inferred. Supergiant's engine lineage is 2D (XNA → MonoGame fork for Transistor: "our proprietary 2D engine, an evolution of the tools ... we created to make Bastion") [Supergiant Transistor FAQ]; Bastion is "a two-dimensional game with an isometric camera and a hand-painted art style" [Wikipedia: Bastion].

**Dofus / Wakfu (Ankama).** 2D isometric tile games with grid tactical combat. Dofus shipped in Flash with isometric grid-based turn-based combat; Dofus 3.0 (2024) moved to Unity [Wikipedia: Dofus]. Wakfu is Java, "isometric graphics," turn-based grid combat, and unlike Dofus's static screen-per-map camera, Wakfu's camera scrolls [Wikipedia: Wakfu]. I could not reach a developer-authored description of the cell-index → screen mapping (the SFML thread on the Dofus map format was blocked); the standard practice for this lineage is the diamond-grid formula in §2 with a per-cell height offset (inferred).

**Into the Breach (Subset).** Custom C++/Lua engine on SDL2 [Steam engine thread]; "written in C++ ... Lua scripting," all pixel art in Photoshop [Game Developer: Road to the IGF]. 8x8 grid, 2D sprites (dev-stated grid; sprite isometric inferred from art).

**Final Fantasy Tactics (1997) / Tactics Ogre.** 3D tile maps + 2D sprites, rotatable. FFT battle maps are 3D polygons; "character sprites are ... flat quad polygons with transparent textures, so they are affected by the 3D camera"; the map stores "the height of each corner of each tile (as well as ... vertical extents and walkability), and interpolates those values to determine the bottom-center point of the sprite" [GameDev.net thread summary]. The renderer reads pitch/yaw/roll every frame, with 4 yaw stops and 2 pitches, and an overhead pitch "causes sprite draw order issues, character bodies disappearing into higher terrain" [FFHacktics wiki] -- i.e. billboard sprites in a depth-buffered 3D scene, and the sprite/terrain depth fight is exactly the known failure. Wikipedia classes FFT as "hybrid (3D with parallel projection)". Tactics Ogre: Reborn keeps 2D sprites on 3D-rendered battlefields [nintendoeverything].

**Octopath Traveler / Triangle Strategy (HD-2D, Unreal Engine 4).** 3D tile environments + billboard pixel sprites + perspective camera with tilt-shift/DoF. HD-2D "combines ... pixel art and billboard sprites, with fully three-dimensional environments," with "dynamic lighting, depth of field, and tilt-shift" [Wikipedia: HD-2D]; Acquire "relied heavily on Unreal Engine 4" [same]. Triangle Strategy producer Asano: "Octopath Traveler had a fixed camera, but in tactical RPGs, you need to be able to rotate the map 360 degrees ... make maps ... look good from all angles" [Nintendo Life] -- dev-stated confirmation that a grid-tactics HD-2D game is a real rotating 3D scene with sprites, not a fixed-angle fake.

**Monument Valley (ustwo, Unity).** 3D geometry, orthographic camera, screen-space adjacency for navigation: nodes are "ordered ... in depth order from the game camera," walkable adjacency is decided from the camera's view rather than 3D connectivity, and "Point lights won't work, because here, things that are next to each other aren't next to each other" [Game Developer / Peter Pashley]. The cleanest demonstration that ortho-3D lets the screen, not the world, define the puzzle.

**Eastward / Stardew (top-down, not iso).** Eastward is "a combination of 3D lighting and retro-pixel style artwork" on a custom engine [Chucklefish]; relevant only as evidence that a 2D-looking game can carry a real depth/normal pipeline. Godot's Y-sort recipe describes the shared rule for all three-quarter 2D: the object's "position is aligned with the bottom of the sprite" [KidsCanCode].

## 2. The math

**2:1 dimetric, not 30°.** The pixel-art "isometric" tile is 2:1: "for every two pixels we move horizontally, we move exactly one pixel vertically," on-screen axis angle = arctan(0.5) ≈ 26.565° [Pikuma; Wikipedia]. True isometric has the three axes at 120° with a 30° on-screen angle and requires a camera pitch of 35.264° [Wikipedia]. Clint Bellanger's Blender recipe makes the camera side explicit: orthographic camera, rotation "(60, 0, 45) for video-game style isometric (tiles that are 2x wide as they are tall)" and "(54.736, 0, 45) for true engineering isometric (but jagged edges due to the angles)" [Bellanger: intro; tiles]. In Blender's convention RotX=60 is 60° from straight down, i.e. the camera looks 30° down from the horizon. **Derivation (mine):** a ground square seen from yaw 45° and pitch θ above the horizon has screen width d and screen height d·sinθ; sin 30° = 0.5 gives exactly 2:1, so the 30° number belongs to the camera pitch and 26.565° to the tile edge on screen -- both are correct, they name different angles. Vertical world units project at cos θ (0.866 at 30°), which is why an elevation step of one tile-height looks slightly compressed and why Unity's Z-as-Y bias is not 0.5 (see §3).

**Grid ↔ screen (2D model).** Bellanger's standard form, tile 128x64: `screen.x = (map.x - map.y) * TILE_WIDTH_HALF; screen.y = (map.x + map.y) * TILE_HEIGHT_HALF` and inverse `map.x = (screen.x / TILE_WIDTH_HALF + screen.y / TILE_HEIGHT_HALF) / 2; map.y = (screen.y / TILE_HEIGHT_HALF - screen.x / TILE_WIDTH_HALF) / 2` [Bellanger: math]; Pikuma gives the same with an origin offset and notes the inverse is just the inverse of the 2x2 matrix [Pikuma]. Elevation is a screen-Y subtraction of `h * TILE_HEIGHT_HALF` (or whatever your vertical unit is) applied after projection; it does not change the grid inverse, which is why screen→grid picking on elevated tiles needs either an id buffer or an iterative height-aware lookup (inferred; the Godot demo's "tiles have different vertical offsets" is the same idea [Godot demo README]).

**Y-sort keys and the isometric sorting problem.** The base rule: draw in order of screen-Y of the feet point (grid `x + y`, tie-broken by x); Godot's `y_sort_enabled` renders "nodes with a higher Y position ... in front of nodes with a lower Y position" [Godot CanvasItem]. Ties on Y are not free: a Godot proposal shows that same-Y tiles "must be drawn from left to right to occlude each other naturally" in some layouts and right-to-left in others, hence `x_draw_order_reversed` [Godot proposal #2838; TileMapLayer]. The rule breaks the moment an object spans more than one tile: "Simple y-coordinate sorting fails for objects larger than a single tile ... When creeps start moving between tiles the rendering all gets messed up" [Mazebert]. The standard fixes, in rising cost: (1) split long sprites into per-tile pieces (Godot's proposal thread and the Paper2D thread both land here); (2) sort by a feet/pivot point, which Unity exposes as Sprite Sort Point = Pivot [Unity: sort point] but which "fails easily on irregular shaped sprites" [Medium/RodFernandez]; (3) topological sort of 3D AABBs: build a "directed graph of the boxes with edges pointing to boxes that are behind it," the in-front test being "lower x is in front, lower y is in front, higher z is in front" when the ranges are disjoint on that axis, then DFS; cycles ("three boxes intertwined") must be broken by splitting or clipping, detectable with Tarjan's SCC [Shaun Lebron]; Mazebert ships exactly this (`if (b.minX < a.maxX && b.minY < a.maxY && b.minZ < a.maxZ) a.behind.add(b)`), O(n²), fine for 100-200 sprites [Mazebert]; (4) a real depth buffer, which Pillars/Disco reach by pre-rendering depth for the painting and letting the GPU do the sort [Obsidian; ZA/UM].

**The 3D camera.** Yaw 45° around +Y, pitch -30° (2:1 dimetric) or -35.264° (true iso), orthographic. That is Bellanger's Blender camera translated into an engine view [Bellanger: tiles]; Godot's Camera3D `orthogonal` projection with `size` is the same thing [Godot forum / TweakGame]. Elevation is then free (it is just Y), picking is a ray or an id buffer, and there is no sorting problem because there is a depth buffer (inferred from the model).

## 3. Engine tutorials and official docs

**Unity.** Official Isometric Tilemap docs teach model (a): create an "Isometric Tilemap" or "Isometric Z as Y Tilemap"; set Transparency Sort Mode = Custom Axis with axis (0,1,0) so higher-Y renderers draw first, and (0,1,-0.26) for Z-as-Y so that "Tiles with higher Z positions [are] drawn first" [Unity manual: create isometric tilemap]. The blog gives the derivation: cell size Y = base height / width = 0.5 for a 64x32 tile; the Z bias = "-0.5 x grid Y, minus 0.01" = -0.26 [Unity blog]. Tilemap Renderer must be in Individual mode for per-tile sorting; Chunk mode "does not support dynamic sorting with other 2D objects" [Unity blog]. Sprite Sort Point defaults to Center; Pivot mode sorts by the pivot's world position [Unity manual: sort point]. Pixel Perfect Camera is orthogonal to the iso question (it scales the viewport to a Reference Resolution and PPU) [Unity manual: 2D Pixel Perfect]. Verdict: Unity's *official* iso story is 2D sprites + Y-axis sort + a Z bias; elevation is a hack (Z offsets Y), and multi-tile objects are your problem. Pillars/Disco/Monument Valley all used Unity for model (b) instead, with custom shaders.

**Godot 4.** TileSet tile shape "Isometric," plus Tile Layout / Tile Offset Axis [Godot: using tilesets]; per-tile Y Sort Origin "allows using layers as if they were on different height" [same]; TileMapLayer `y_sort_origin` "is added to each tile's Y-sort origin value ... to fake a different height level" and `x_draw_order_reversed` for the same-Y tie [Godot: TileMapLayer]. Y-sort is a CanvasItem property [Godot: CanvasItem]. The official 2D Isometric demo is "a TileMap in which the tiles have different vertical offsets," bodies at the feet, blob shadow, PointLight2D shadows [Godot demo README]. Gotchas from the forums: sorting looks broken unless the texture origin is at the base [Stephan Bester]; Godot "does not automatically sort between layers" so multi-layer elevation is manual [Godot forums]; same-Y draw order needed a proposal [#2838]. The 3D route is Camera3D in Orthogonal projection, taught only by community devlogs [YouTube devlog; TweakGame]. Verdict: Godot documents model (a) thoroughly and honestly, including its elevation limits; model (b) is unofficial but trivial.

**Unreal.** Paper2D's isometric tilemap sorts by layer depth, and the moment a sprite must be both in front of and behind the same layer it fails: "higher layers that are behind the sprite will render on top of the sprite and if I set the sprite to be higher it will render on top of the higher layers" -- unresolved [Epic forums: Paper2D]. The common pattern is instead 3D tiles + orthographic CameraComponent, and Epic's orthographic camera page now supports "Lumen, Nanite, Virtual Shadow Maps, TSR, reflections, volumetrics, path tracing" but warns "Unreal Engine's priority has always been perspective view cameras," and adds Auto Calculate Ortho Planes / Auto Plane Shift / Update Ortho Planes because ortho near/far and view origin misbehave "due to the lack of depth" [Epic: Orthographic Camera]. Community history shows the pitfalls: shadows clip or vanish above an Ortho Width threshold, fixed by moving the camera back and raising Ortho Width or offsetting the near plane [Epic forums: shadows 5.3; no shadows]. HD-2D-style setups (Octopath/Triangle Strategy) are 3D scenes with a *perspective* camera and billboard sprites [Wikipedia: HD-2D; Nintendo Life]. Verdict: Unreal teaches (b) or (c) on real 3D geometry; its 2D sprite path is not a serious iso option.

**Engine-agnostic.** Bellanger's three articles (intro/math/tiles) are the canonical 2:1 formulas plus the exact Blender camera [clintbellanger.net]; Pikuma restates the projection math with the matrix inverse [Pikuma]; Shaun Lebron's "Drawing isometric boxes in the correct order" is the reference for topological sorting [Lebron]; Mazebert's post is a shipped implementation of it [Mazebert]; Wikipedia's isometric-graphics article is the best single source on which classic games used dimetric vs trimetric vs oblique [Wikipedia]. Red Blob Games is the hex-grid reference (cube/axial coordinates) and only matters if the grid becomes hex [Red Blob]. Talks: Obsidian Update #79 (the video Disco's TA cites) [Obsidian]; Raidma's GDC 2020 "Turpentine Fumes and Shaders" [Game Developer]; Pashley's Nordic Game 2014 Monument Valley talk [Game Developer]; Epic's Octopath/Octopath II spotlights [Unreal Engine site, 403 at fetch time].

## 4. Synthesis

**(a) 2D plane + 2:1 sprites + coordinate rule.** Users: Diablo 2, Dofus/Wakfu, Into the Breach, Bastion/Transistor/Hades (custom 2D engines), Unity and Godot official tilemap paths. Buys: cheapest art pipeline (paint or pre-render tiles/sprites, no lighting model to match), pixel-exact look, trivial 2D physics, a grid ↔ screen rule that is two multiplies. Costs: no occlusion for free -- Y-sort with tie rules, feet pivots, sprite splitting, or an O(n²) topological sort; elevation is a screen-Y offset hack that breaks the picking inverse (needs an id buffer or height-aware lookup); lighting/shadows are fake (Godot 2D lights, blob shadows); no camera freedom at all (rotate = re-author art). Engine needs: sprite batch with a sort key, a Y-sort pass with stable ties, a tilemap layer with per-tile origin offsets, id-buffer picking. Physics stays 2D on the grid plane.

**(b) Orthographic 3D camera on the diagonal over a 3D ground.** Users: Pillars/Deadfire, Disco Elysium, Monument Valley (all Unity ortho), FFT in spirit, and every Unreal "3D tiles + ortho camera" project. Buys: real depth buffer (the isometric sorting problem disappears), real elevation, real lighting/shadows, ray or id-buffer picking that just works on slopes, and a fixed, controlled look; can still host billboard sprites for characters (FFT) and painted backgrounds via depth-writing proxy geometry (Disco). Costs: an art pipeline that must agree on the camera (pre-rendered sprites must be rendered at exactly the same 45°/30° or they mismatch), ortho-specific renderer pitfalls (near/far, shadow cascades, view-origin -- Epic's list), and an angle that must be frozen early because every asset bakes it in. Engine needs: orthographic Camera3D with size/near/far, billboard sprite node with a feet-pivot and depth write, a way to write pre-rendered depth into the buffer, shadow maps that survive ortho.

**(c) Narrow-FOV perspective.** Users: Diablo 3/4, Path of Exile 1/2, DOS2/BG3, Octopath/Triangle Strategy (HD-2D). Buys: everything in (b) plus camera freedom (rotate, zoom, tilt, tactical view), diorama depth cues (tilt-shift, DoF), and a renderer path that is what every modern engine optimises for. Costs: nothing is "on grid" on screen -- sprites and tiles distort toward the edges (D3's complaint), pixel-art sprites cannot stay pixel-exact, 2D tricks (pre-rendered backgrounds) become impossible, and every asset must survive many angles (Triangle Strategy's "make maps look good from all angles" cost). Engine needs: the normal 3D renderer, billboard sprites that face the camera, a camera rig with FOV/pitch/yaw limits, mesh picking.

**Recommendation for Aphelyon (grid tactics, sprite characters, Source-2-style renderer later).** Take (b) with the camera parameterised so it can become (c): a 3D world with the ground and grid in the XZ plane at +Y up, an orthographic camera at yaw 45° / pitch -30°, and characters as depth-writing billboard sprites anchored at a feet point on the tile centre (FFT's model), with the tile grid stored as world coordinates plus a per-corner height. The 2:1 look is then a property of the camera, not of the art rule; the sorting problem is deleted by the depth buffer; picking uses the existing id buffer; and the later deferred renderer lights meshes and sprites the same way, which is what the Source 2 target needs and what model (a) can never give. Pre-render the sprites at exactly that camera (Bellanger's 60/0/45) so they match. Keep the perspective toggle on the same rig for the HD-2D look if wanted. **The decision that has to be made first** is the coordinate one: whether the game's world coordinates are 2D screen-plane coordinates with a fake depth key (model (a), and the current Ortho2D XY camera), or 3D coordinates on a ground plane with the camera doing the projection (models (b)/(c)). Everything downstream -- grid → world mapping, sorting vs depth buffer, how elevation exists, what the picker returns, what the physics plane is -- follows from that one choice, and it cannot be retrofitted cheaply because the tile data, the sprite pivots and the camera all bake it in.
